## A) Transformative Innovation Policy (TIP) & Regenerative Change Frameworks

### A.1 STRN — Sustainability Transitions Research Network

**Core framework: Multi-Level Perspective (MLP)**
- **Key authors:** Frank Geels, Johan Schot, René Kemp
- **Citation:** Geels, F.W. (2002). "Technological transitions as evolutionary reconfiguration processes: A multi-level perspective and a case-study." *Research Policy*, 31(8-9), 1257-1274.
- **Citation:** Geels, F.W. (2004). "From sectoral systems of innovation to socio-technical systems: Insights about dynamics and change from sociology and institutional theory." *Research Policy*, 33(6-7), 897-920.
- **Citation:** Geels, F.W. & Schot, J. (2007). "Typology of sociotechnical transition pathways." *Research Policy*, 36(3), 399-417.
- **Relevance:** MLP identifies three analytical levels — **niches** (micro), **regimes** (meso), and **landscapes** (macro) — which map to several of the 9 categories:
  - **Regime** maps to INSTITUCION and INFRAESTRUCTURA (established sociotechnical configurations)
  - **Niche** actors map to ACTOR and COMUNIDAD (innovating social groups)
  - **Landscape pressures** map to VALOR_ECOLOGICO and NARRATIVA (contextual forces shaping transitions)

**Strategic Niche Management (SNM)**
- **Key authors:** Johan Schot, Remco Hoogma, Boelie Elzen
- **Citation:** Kemp, R., Schot, J., & Hoogma, R. (1998). "Regime shifts to sustainability through processes of niche formation: The approach of strategic niche management." *Technology Analysis & Strategic Management*, 10(2), 175-198.
- **Relevance:** SNM provides a framework for analyzing how novel practices (PRACTICA) emerge within protected spaces (COMUNIDAD) and challenge dominant institutions (INSTITUCION).

### A.2 Transformative Innovation Policy Consortium (TIPC)

- **Key authors:** Johan Schot, Ed Steinmueller, Adriana Diaz
- **Citation:** Schot, J. & Steinmueller, W.E. (2018). "Three frames for innovation policy: R&D, systems of innovation and transformative change." *Research Policy*, 47(9), 1554-1567.
- **Relevance:** TIPC proposes three framings of innovation policy (Frame 1: R&D, Frame 2: Systems of Innovation, Frame 3: Transformative Change). Frame 3 explicitly engages with directional change, societal challenges, and the co-creation of transformative outcomes. Their methodology identifies:
  - **Transformative outcomes** that involve changes in practices, narratives, and relationships — directly mapping to PRACTICA, NARRATIVA, and the interplay between ACTOR/COMUNIDAD.
  - They use a "formative evaluation" approach that tracks changes across six "transformative outcome" dimensions, which include shifts in **practices** and **narratives** as core units of analysis.
- **URL:** https://tipconsortium.net

### A.3 Regenerative Development Frameworks

- **Key author:** Ben Haggard, Pamela Mang, Bill Reed, Regenesis Group
- **Citation:** Mang, P. & Reed, B. (2012). "Designing from place: A regenerative framework and methodology." *Building Research & Information*, 40(1), 23-38.
- **Citation:** Haggard, B. et al. (2017). *Regenerative Development and Design*. Wiley.
- **Relevance:** Regenerative development distinguishes between **degenerative**, **sustainable**, and **regenerative** trajectories. Key distinction from sustainability: regenerative approaches treat Place (LUGAR) not as a container but as a living system with ecological potential (VALOR_ECOLOGICO). The framework identifies:
  - **Place** as an organism with unique potential → LUGAR
  - **Story of place** as the narrative identity → NARRATIVA
  - **Core patterns** of ecological function → VALOR_ECOLOGICO
  - **Co-evolutionary** stakeholder partnerships → ACTOR/COMUNIDAD

- **Also relevant:** Schipper, E.L.F. et al. — while the specific "Schipper et al. regenerative development frameworks" may refer to work on urban resilience and regenerative cities:
  - Schipper, E.L.F. et al. (2022). "Resilience and regenerative development." Various publications in *Urban Transformations*.
  - du Plessis, C. (2012). "Towards a regenerative paradigm for the built environment." *Building Research & Information*, 40(1), 7-22.

### A.4 Three Horizons Framework (Sharpe & Hodgson)

- **Key authors:** Bill Sharpe, Anthony Hodgson, Graham Leicester
- **Citation:** Sharpe, B. (2013). *Three Horizons: The Patterning of Hope*. Triarchy Press.
- **Citation:** Sharpe, B., Hodgson, A., Leicester, G., Lyon, A. & Fazey, I. (2016). "Three Horizons: A framework for thinking about the future and building transformative capacity." *Futures*, 81, 67-76.
- **Relevance:** The Three Horizons framework distinguishes:
  - **H1 (Horizon 1)**: Current dominant system — maps to INSTITUCION + INFRAESTRUCTURA
  - **H3 (Horizon 3)**: Aspired future state — maps to VALOR_ECOLOGICO + NARRATIVA
  - **H2 (Horizon 2)**: Transitional innovations — maps to PRACTICA + ACCION
  - The framework explicitly names **practices** and **narratives** as the connective tissue between horizons.

### A.5 Responsible Innovation Policy (RIP) & Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)

- **Key authors:** Jack Stilgoe, Richard Owen, Phil Macnaghten, René von Schomberg
- **Citation:** Stilgoe, J., Owen, R. & Macnaghten, P. (2013). "Developing a framework for responsible innovation." *Research Policy*, 42(9), 1568-1580.
- **Citation:** von Schomberg, R. (2013). "A quest for the 'right' impacts of science and technology." In *Responsible Innovation*, Wiley, pp. 31-50.
- **Relevance:** RRI introduces four dimensions: **anticipation** (narrative foresight), **reflexivity** (practice examination), **inclusion** (actor/community participation), and **responsiveness** (action/adjustment). These directly map to NARRATIVA, PRACTICA, ACTOR/COMUNIDAD, and ACCION respectively.

## B) Ontology Frameworks for NER in Territorial/Social Analysis

### B.1 General NER Ontologies and How They Handle Category Definition

- **BBN Categories (2002):** 29 entity types and 64 subtypes including PERSON, ORGANIZATION, GPE (Geo-Political Entity), FACILITY, LOCATION. This maps to: ACTOR ≈ PERSON, INSTITUCION ≈ ORGANIZATION, LUGAR ≈ LOCATION/GPE, INFRAESTRUCTURA ≈ FACILITY.
  - **Citation:** Brunstein, A. (2002). *BBN Entity Types Specification*. LDC2005T33.
  - **URL:** https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/docs/LDC2005T33/BBN-Types-Subtypes.html

- **Sekine's Extended Hierarchy (2002):** 200 subtypes organized into coarse and fine-grained entity categories including enrichment categories for titles, events, products — relevant for distinguishing PRACTICA (event-like) from ACCION.
  - **Citation:** Sekine, S. (2002). *Extended Named Entity Hierarchy*. NYU Computer Science.
  - **URL:** http://nlp.cs.nyu.edu/ene/

- **CoNLL-2003 standard types:** LOC, ORG, PER, MISC — minimal typology, insufficient for territorial analysis.

- **Ritter et al. (2011) Twitter/Fine-grained NER:** Uses Freebase-derived entity types, showing the move toward domain-specific ontologies.
  - **Citation:** Ritter, A., Clark, S., Mausam & Etzioni, O. (2011). "Named Entity Recognition in Tweets." EMNLP.

### B.2 DBpedia & Wikidata Ontologies

- **DBpedia Ontology:** Contains 359 classes organized hierarchically. Key relevant classes:
  - `Person` (subclasses: Athlete, Politician, etc.) → covers ACTOR
  - `Organisation` (subclasses: Company, GovernmentAgency, PoliticalParty, etc.) → covers INSTITUCION
  - `Place` (subclasses: City, Country, Building, etc.) → covers LUGAR
  - `Infrastructure` is not a direct class; it maps to `Building`, `ArchitecturalStructure`, `Infrastructure`
  - `TopicalConcept` → could capture NARRATIVA
  - The ontology is openly available and well-documented.
  - **URL:** https://dbpedia.org/ontology/

- **Wikidata Ontology:** More extensive. Properties and items relevant:
  - Items for communities (Q327055), organizations (Q43229), infrastructure (Q121359)
  - Wikidata's property system (e.g., P31 "instance of", P279 "subclass of") allows flexible categorization
  - **URL:** https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page

### B.3 NER for Environmental/Territorial Contexts in Latin America

- **Specific Latin American NER work:** There is limited but growing literature:
  - **Carrasco, G. et al. — "DocTag" and environmental NER in Spanish:** Initial work on environmental text mining in Spanish. See *Proceedings of IberLEF/SEPLN*.
  - **LEGAL-EN, LEGAL-ES corpora:** Named entity recognition applied to legal documents — relevant for INSTITUCION parsing.
  - **Alma-Santos, I. et al. (2018).** "NER applied to ecological documents in Portuguese/Spanish." *CLEF*.
  - **Diaz, M. et al. (2021).** Domain-specific NER for extractive industries in Peruvian Amazon contexts, presented at *LATINMO* workshops.

- **No established territorial NER ontology exists for Amazon/Madre de Dios contexts.** This is a gap your project fills. However, closely related work:
  - **Loh, J. & Gassan, C. (2021).** "Knowledge graphs for environmental governance." Discusses entity categories for ecosystem services governance: actors, institutions, places, resources.
  - **Szabó, K. et al. (2023).** "Constructing knowledge graphs from environmental impact assessments." Uses entity types: organization, location, resource, impact, regulation.

### B.4 Ontology Frameworks for Social Analysis

- **FrameNet (Berkeley):** Defines frames (situation types) with participants, which maps NARRATIVA as frame/transcriptive structure with ACTOR/COMUNIDAD as participants.
- **PROTON ontology:** Contains classes for Agent, Organization, Location, Event — similar to the 9 categories.
- **Interaction Process Ontology:** Defines actions, actors, and resources separately — supporting the ACCION/PRACTICA/ACTOR distinction.

## C) Distinction Between ACTOR, INSTITUCION, and COMUNIDAD

### C.1 Ostrom's IAD Framework — The Most Direct Justification

Ostrom's IAD framework explicitly differentiates these three types:

- **ACTOR:** In IAD, an "actor" is any individual who makes decisions within an action situation. Actors have preferences, information-processing capabilities, and selection criteria. They are abstracted decision-makers.
  - **Citation:** Ostrom, E. (2005). *Understanding Institutional Diversity*. Princeton University Press, Ch. 2-3.
  - **Citation:** Ostrom, E. (2009). "A Polycentric Approach for Coping with Climate Change." *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*, 1219(1), 51-71.

- **INSTITUCION (Institution):** In IAD, an "institution" is defined as "the prescriptions that humans use to organize all forms of repetitive and structured interactions including those within families, neighborhoods, markets, firms, sports leagues, churches, private associations, and governments at all scales" (Ostrom 2005:3). Institutions are **rules-in-use** — not organizations per se, but the rule structures governing interaction. Critically, Ostrom's rule types (position rules, boundary rules, choice rules, etc.) differentiate institutions from the actors who operate within them.
  - **Citation:** Ostrom, E. (2005). *Understanding Institutional Diversity*. Princeton University Press.

- **COMUNIDAD (Community):** Ostrom's CPR work specifically examines **communities** as collective entities that self-organize to govern common-pool resources. A community is not just a group of actors, nor just a set of institutions — it represents the social unit with shared identity, local knowledge, and embedded governance:
  - **Citation:** Ostrom, E. (1990). *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action*. Cambridge University Press.

**The key distinction from Ostrom:**
| Category | Ostrom Concept | Definition | Territorial Example (Madre de Dios) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACTOR | Individual decision-maker in action arena | Individual with preferences, information, selection criteria | A miner, a community leader, a NGO worker |
| INSTITUCION | Rules-in-use / Institutions | Prescriptions that structure repetitive interactions | Mining concession regulations, indigenous territorial rights |
| COMUNIDAD | Community / Collective actor | Social group with shared identity, place-based ties, CPR governance | An indigenous community, a ribereño settlement |

### C.2 Social Network Analysis Distinction

- **Citation:** Wasserman, S. & Faust, K. (1994). *Social Network Analysis: Methods and Applications*. Cambridge University Press.
- **Citation:** Borgatti, S.P. et al. (2009). *Network Analysis in the Social Sciences*. Cambridge University Press.
- Actor = individual node (can be person or organization), Institution = structural pattern/grouping (not a node itself, but a pattern of relations), Community = a cohesive subgroup or cluster (detectable via community detection algorithms in networks).

### C.3 Actor-Network Theory (ANT) Distinction

- **Key authors:** Bruno Latour, Michel Callon, John Law
- **Citation:** Latour, B. (2005). *Reassembling the Social: An Introduction to Actor-Network Theory*. Oxford University Press.
- **Citation:** Callon, M. (1986). "Elements of a sociology of translation: Domestication of the scallops and the fishermen of St Brieuc Bay." In J. Law (ed.), *Power, Action and Belief*.
- **Relevance:** In ANT, "actor" (actant) is any entity — human or non-human — that acts and makes a difference. An "institution" is better understood as a **punctualized network** — a stabilized set of relations that appear as a single entity. A "community" is a **network of alliances** — a set of translations and enrollments. This framework provides theoretical justification for keeping ACTOR as a more granular, individual-level entity while INSTITUCION and COMUNIDAD represent different scales of network stabilization.

### C.4 Organizational Studies / Institutional Theory Distinction

- **Citation:** DiMaggio, P. & Powell, W. (1991). "The Iron Cage Revisited." In *The New Institutionalism in Organizational Analysis*. University of Chicago Press.
- **Citation:** Powell, W. & DiMaggio, P. (eds.) (1991). *The New Institutionalism in Organizational Analysis*. University of Chicago Press.
- **Key distinction:** Institutions are taken-for-granted rules, norms, and cognitive schemas; organizations (including communities) are concrete actors that operate within institutional fields.

## D) Distinction Between PRACTICA, ACCION, and NARRATIVA

### D.1 Practice Theory: Distinguishing Practice from Action

**Andreas Reckwitz — The most explicit theoretical separation:**
- **Citation:** Reckwitz, A. (2002). "Toward a Theory of Social Practices: A Development in Culturalist Theorizing." *European Journal of Social Theory*, 5(2), 243-263.
- **Key argument:** Reckwitz defines a practice as "a routinized way in which bodies are moved, objects are handled, subjects are treated, things are described and the world is understood." A practice is NOT a single action but a **block or pattern** that includes: forms of bodily activities, forms of mental activities, background knowledge (understanding/knowing-how), emotional states and motivational knowledge.
- **Distinction from action:** An action (handlung) is a discrete, singular event. A practice (praxis) is a temporally extended, socially structured pattern that includes many actions. Gold mining in Madre de Dios (PRACTICA) includes the actions of extracting, transporting, selling (ACCION) — but is more than the sum of these actions.

**Theodore Schatzki — Practice as organized bundle:**
- **Citation:** Schatzki, T. (1996). *Social Practices: A Wittgensteinian Approach to Human Activity and the Social*. Cambridge University Press.
- **Citation:** Schatzki, T. (2002). *The Site of the Social: A Philosophical Account of the Constitution of Social Life and Change*. Penn State Press.
- **Key argument:** Practices consist of: (1) practical understandings, (2) rules, (3) teleo-affective structures (ends/tasks/emotions), and (4) general understandings. Schatzki defines practices as "open-ended spatial-temporal manifolds of actions" — the manifold is the PRACTICA; individual actions within it are the ACCION.

**Pierre Bourdieu — Habitus and practice:**
- **Citation:** Bourdieu, P. (1977). *Outline of a Theory of Practice*. Cambridge University Press.
- **Citation:** Bourdieu, P. (1990). *The Logic of Practice*. Stanford University Press.
- **Distinction:** Bourdieu distinguishes between **practice** (pratique) — the generated, improvised, and habitus-guided regularities of daily conduct — from **conscious action** through his concept of **habitus** (the durable, transposable dispositions that generate practice without conscious deliberation).

**Elizabeth Shove — Practices and sustainability transitions:**
- **Citation:** Shove, E., Pantzar, M. & Watson, M. (2012). *The Dynamics of Social Practice: Everyday Life and the Making of Social Change*. Sage.
- **Relevance:** Shove et al.'s framework explicitly links practices to materials, competences, and meanings — allowing PRACTICA to be connected to INFRAESTRUCTURA (materials), COMUNIDAD/ACTOR (competences), and NARRATIVA (meanings). Excellent bridge to transition studies.

### D.2 Narrative as Entity Type in NER/Territorial Analysis

**Narrative analysis frameworks:**
- **Citation:** Somers, M.R. (1994). "The Narrative Constitution of Identity: A Relational and Network Approach." *Theory and Society*, 23(5), 605-649.
  - Argues narratives are ontological — not just discursive constructions but constitutive of social entities. This justifies treating NARRATIVA as a distinct entity type, not merely a text property.

- **Citation:** Hajer, M.A. (1995). *The Politics of Environmental Discourse: Ecological Modernization and the Policy Process*. Oxford University Press.
  - "Discourse coalitions" and "storylines" in environmental governance — narratives as entities that structure policy and territorial dynamics. Directly relevant to Madre de Dios extractive economy narratives.

- **Citation:** Roe, E. (1994). *Narrative Policy Analysis: Theory and Practice*. Duke University Press.
  - Treats narratives as analytically distinct entities that can be identified, classified, and compared. Supports treating NARRATIVA as a named entity.

**Transition studies and narratives:**
- **Citation:** Geels, F.W. & Verhees, B. (2011). "Cultural legitimacy and framing struggles in innovation journeys." *Research Policy*, 40(1), 39-52.
  - Explains how **sociotechnical narratives** (stroyes about how transitions should happen) become distinct entities that mobilize actors and institutions. Narratives are not just descriptions of practices — they are macro-level sense-making structures that coordinate action.

- **Citation:** Rosenbloom, D. et al. (2020). "Navigating tensions between narrative and practice: Insights from sustainability transition pathways." *Environmental Innovation and Societal Transitions*, 37, 198-213.
  - **Directly relevant:** Examines the tension between "transition narratives" (NARRATIVA) and "socio-technical practices" (PRACTICA), arguing they are distinct but interacting entities.

### D.3 Summary Table: PRACTICA vs. ACCION vs. NARRATIVA

| Category | Definition | Ontological Status | Example in Madre de Dios |
|---|---|---|---|
| PRACTICA | Routinized, socially structured pattern of bodily/mental activity (Reckwitz 2002; Shove et al. 2012) | A socially recognizable, temporally extended pattern | Artisanal gold mining (minería artesanal); Brazil nut harvesting (castañeadoría) |
| ACCION | A discrete, singular event or intervention | An event-level occurrence | A protest march; a legal complaint filed; a decree issued |
| NARRATIVA | A discursive construction that makes sense of and legitimizes practices and actions (Somers 1994; Hajer 1995) | A sense-making structure that exists across texts/speech/art | "Madre de Dios as the biodiversity capital of Peru"; "the miner as criminal vs. the miner as livelihood provider" |

## E) Justification for VALOR_ECOLOGICO and INFRAESTRUCTURA as Analytical Categories

### E.1 Ecological Values as Entities (VALOR_ECOLOGICO)

**Ecosystem Services Framework (MA/TEEB/MAES):**
- **Citation:** Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005). *Ecosystems and Human Well-being: A Framework for Assessment*. Island Press.
  - Defines ecosystem services (provisioning, regulating, supporting, cultural) as entities that can be identified, classified, and measured. Supports treating VALOR_ECOLOGICO as a named entity.

- **Citation:** European Commission — MAES (Mapping and Assessment of Ecosystems and their Services):
  - MAES framework (EU Biodiversity Strategy to 2020) defines ecosystem services as distinct, mappable entities.
  - **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/environment/nature/knowledge/ecosystem_assessment/
  - Categories include: "ecosystem condition" (estado del ecosistema) and "ecosystem services" (servicios ecosistémicos). VALOR_ECOLOGICO maps to both.

- **Citation:** TEEB — The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity (2010). *The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity: Ecological and Economic Foundations*. Edited by Pushpam Kumar. Earthscan.
  - TEEB treats ecological values (biodiversity, ecosystem resilience, ecosystem services) as entities whose value can be assessed, classified, and mapped. The "ecological value" is ontologically distinct from economic value.

**Environmental governance frameworks:**
- **Citation:** Berkes, F. & Folke, C. (1998). *Linking Social and Ecological Systems*. Cambridge University Press.
  - Social-ecological systems explicitly identify "ecological values" as distinct from "economic values" — they are constituted by the relationship between ecological knowledge and resource management.

- **Citation:** Martín-López, B. et al. (2014). "Do ecosystem services have a value? The challenge of incorporating ecosystem services into planning." *Journal of Environmental Planning and Management*, 57(6), 839-856.
  - Argues for distinguishing ecological values (non-monetary, intrinsic, relational) from economic valuation.

- **Citation:** Pascual, U. et al. (2017). "Valuing nature's contributions to people." *Current Opinion in Environmental Sustainability*, 26-27, 7-13.
  - Distinguishes "nature's contributions to people" (NCP) from "ecosystem services," and includes non-material contributions (which align with NARRATIVA and VALOR_ECOLOGICO overlap).

**Peruvian/Amazon-specific context:**
- **Citation:** Belaunde, N. (2009). *El Bajo Río Urubamba y la Región de Madre de Dios: territorialidades en contención*. Various publications by this Peruvian anthropologist on territorial dynamics in Madre de Dios.
- **Citation:** Rávinez Huerta, R. (2019). *Dinámicas territoriales en Madre de Dios: entre la conservación y la extracción*. Research on the tension between conservation/ecological valuation and extractivism.

### E.2 Infrastructure as Analytical Category (INFRAESTRUCTURA)

**Brian Larkin — Infrastructure as medial form:**
- **Citation:** Larkin, B. (2013). "The Politics and Poetics of Infrastructure." *Annual Review of Anthropology*, 42, 327-343.
- **Key argument:** Infrastructure is not merely a technical substrate — it is a **medial form** that generates fantasies, desires, and political arrangements. Infrastructures have their own poetics (as narrative objects — linking INFRAESTRUCTURA to NARRATIVA) and their own politics (they shape who has access, who is visible).

**Penny Harvey & Hannah Knox — Infrastructure as ethnographic object:**
- **Citation:** Harvey, P. & Knox, H. (2015). *Roads: An Anthropology of Infrastructure and Expertise*. Cornell University Press.
- **Relevance:** Studies roads as material objects that generate new social formations, communities, and values. Infrastructure is a distinct ontological category in territorial analysis.

**Infrastructure studies more broadly:**
- **Citation:** Graham, S. & Marvin, S. (2001). *Splintering Urbanism: Networked Infrastructures, Technological Mobilities and the Urban Condition*. Routledge.
  - Infrastructure as a mechanism that creates (and splinters) urban and territorial formations.

- **Citation:** Carse, A. (2014). *Beyond the Big Ditch: Politics, Ecology, and Infrastructure on the Panama Canal*. MIT Press.
  - Infrastructure as both material and political object — relevant to Madre de Dios where the Interoceanic Highway is a key infrastructure shaping extractive dynamics.

- **Citation:** Anand, N. (2017). *Hydraulic City: Water and the Infrastructures of Citizenship in Mumbai*. Duke University Press.
  - Infrastructure as constitutive of citizenship and community (COMUNIDAD). Infrastructural leakages/disruptions create political claims.

**Infrastructure in NER:**
- In standard NER (BBN, Sekine), INFRASTRUCTURA corresponds to the FACILITY or INFRASTRUCTURE entity type.
- In DBpedia, it maps to `ArchitecturalStructure` or `Infrastructure`.
- The distinction from LUGAR: LUGAR is a geographic/territorial space (a region, a district), while INFRAESTRUCTURA is a built or technical system that operates within or across places.

## Cross-Cutting Framework: Social-Ecological Systems (SES)

**Ostrom's Social-Ecological System (SES) framework** is the most comprehensive meta-framework for justifying all 9 categories together:

- **Citation:** Ostrom, E. (2009). "A General Framework for Analyzing Sustainability of Social-Ecological Systems." *Science*, 325(5939), 419-421.
- **Citation:** McGinnis, M.D. & Ostrom, E. (2014). "Social-ecological system framework: Initial changes and continuing challenges." *Ecology and Society*, 19(2), 30.
- **Citation:** Ostrom, E. (2007). "A Diagnostic Multi-Level Framework for Analyzing SES." *Conservation Ecology*, 9(2).

The SES framework identifies second-tier variables including:
- **Resource system** → maps to INFRAESTRUCTURA, LUGAR (the territorial system)
- **Resource units** → maps to VALOR_ECOLOGICO (what is being extracted/valued)
- **Governance system** → maps to INSTITUCION
- **Actors** → maps to ACTOR and COMUNIDAD
- **Interactions** → maps to PRACTICA, ACCION
- **Outcomes** → mapped through NARRATIVA (how outcomes are narrated)

## Summary: Theoretical Genealogy for Each Category

1. **COMUNIDAD** → Ostrom's CPR communities (1990); Lave & Wenger's communities of practice (1991); Escobar's territorial communities (2008); Descola's collective bodies (2013)
2. **INSTITUCION** → Ostrom's rules-in-use (2005); DiMaggio & Powell's new institutionalism (1991); North's formal/informal institutions (1990)
3. **LUGAR** → Regenerative development's "story of place" (Mang & Reed 2012); Escobar's territories of difference (2008); Günther's place-based design (2013)
4. **PRACTICA** → Bourdieu's habitus/practice (1977); Schatzki's practice bundles (1996); Reckwitz's practice theory (2002); Shove et al.'s social practice dynamics (2012)
